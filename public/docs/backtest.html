Backtest on real data

The backtest screen

Your production history as a chart, with any infrastructure change replayed over it as what would have happened. Open it from the Backtest tab, or go straight to /#backtest.

The chart and the strategies

Everything shares one time axis: what users saw, what the model replays for the system as built, and what each change would have done. The screenshot shows HotROD, one of the three built-in reference systems: 40 minutes of real traffic with a push past capacity, and four strategies replayed over it.

  1. Views

    Every part of Hindcast is one click away: the backtest, the Live board, Lessons, Sandbox, Compare and Studio.

  2. Replay or review

    Replay is this chart. Review is the capacity review of the same system on one screen: headroom, busier days, losing a node, what to commit to, and the fixes that pass. /#backtest/review opens it directly.

  3. Range and resolution

    The whole run, or the last 24 h, 6 h, 1 h or 15 min. ⌘/ctrl + wheel zooms around the cursor. The resolution is the data's own (here a point every 30 s): hover the control to see it. Next to it, load ×1 replays the history at a multiple of its traffic.

  4. p99 latency

    The p99 users saw, measured, as a line over a light area. The dashed grey line is the model replaying the system as built. Each strategy has its own coloured line with a shaded 10–90% band. The dashed red line is the SLO.

  5. Incidents

    Shaded columns are stretches out of SLO. Here, the push to twice the normal load: the as-built system timed out for minutes. Click any moment to open it on the board as a replay.

  6. Errors

    Measured error rate and each strategy's replay. "Clients retry twice, no budget" turns the overload into 100% errors: the retries pile on top of a saturated database lock.

  7. Cost per hour

    From the bill (measured), as built, and per strategy: replica-hours at the billed price, usage-billed requests, data transfer across zones and regions.

  8. Changes tested

    As built and each change (a strategy file): first what it does to the time out of SLO, then what it costs a month, in words. Changes that keep the SLO come first, then the cheapest. Click to focus one, use the swatch to hide it, + test a change to write a new one.

  9. Worst moment, and why

    For the focused strategy: when it was worst and which component limited it, with its load against capacity and its queue. "mysql at 56% of capacity (11.2 req/s on 2 × 9.9 req/s), 85 ms wait". open on board replays that moment.

  10. Model accuracy

    How well this model predicted recorded runs it never saw, check by check, against the targets: p99 within 20%, breaking point within 15%, cost within 10%. Shown with every result, so you know how far to trust it.

  11. The verdict

    One sentence for the focused strategy: what it saves or costs per month and what it would have done to users. It is the same sentence the pull-request check posts.

  12. The numbers

    Net $/month, minutes out of SLO, failed requests, error budget used, peak p99 and replica-hours. Each comes with its 10–90% range from 30 paired draws of the model's uncertain capacities.

  13. System

    Switch between the reference systems, or your own workspace once you've opened files.

  14. Card

    A Studio card of the focused strategy at post size: the chart drawing itself, then the verdict, with the model's measured accuracy.

  15. Replay an outage

    Cloudflare 2019, GitHub 2018, Slack 2021, AWS us-east-1 2025, replayed against this system as built and with each strategy. See public-outage replays.

  16. Open files

    Your own data: an import manifest with its metric, trace and bill files, or a dataset.json and model.json from the CLI, plus strategy files. You can also drop them anywhere on the screen. See bring your data.

The capacity review

The review a platform team does by hand before a launch or a budget round, computed from the same model and history: Review in the bar. It is the same code as hindcast agent review_capacity, so the screen and the Markdown report agree number for number. The screenshot is the Astronomy Shop on Kubernetes.

  1. Five lines first

    Risk, headroom, cost, the one change to make and how far to trust the model, in sentences a lead can repeat. A line about restarts over gRPC is added when the model has single-replica services called that way.

  2. Risk

    High when the recorded day itself left the SLO, medium when a 1.5× or 2× day would, or losing a node would, low when none of them do.

  3. Headroom over the traffic

    The recorded requests per second, the same day at 1.5× and 2× (dashed and dotted), and the dashed red limit: the most the system serves within SLO, and what saturates first. Traffic above the limit is shaded red.

  4. The busiest moment

    Where today's peak sits against that limit. Hover anywhere for the three days' rates at that moment.

  5. Busier days, replayed

    The recorded day and the 1.5× and 2× days, each replayed through the model: minutes out of SLO, failed requests, whether the SLO holds, and the worst moment's cause.

  6. Losing a node at the peak

    Every node's loss is replayed; the worst is listed with the minutes out of SLO and failed requests it adds and the services left with no replica. replay it opens that loss on the replay screen.

  7. The node-loss fix

    Replicas spread over the nodes so losing any one leaves a replica of each, checked by replaying every node's loss again, with what it costs.

  8. Stuck callers, an assumption

    Where the model marks calls as gRPC, the same loss if callers' connections stay broken until they restart (252 s, as measured once on the reference cluster), boxed and labelled an assumption: it is not part of the answer above it. Without gRPC calls it says it was not computed.

  9. What to commit to

    The floor the history never went below, priced on demand, and what a 1- or 3-year commitment on that floor saves. Commit to the floor, not the peak. When costs come from the bill, it says why there is nothing to read.

  10. Limits that bind

    CPU quotas that throttle before the limit, memory near its limit, leaks and how many requests until a container is OOM-killed.

  11. Copy as Markdown

    The same review as the report the CLI and agents write, ready to paste into a ticket.

Fixes and savings that pass

The last part of the review: changes generated from the history (capacity for what ran out of it, cuts for what idled), each backtested over the recorded history and judged. HotROD here. Savings are the cuts that pass (they cost less and add no time out of SLO), listed under the fixes with all of them applied at once and backtested again, since cuts can add up to a problem none of them is alone. HotROD has none: its model misses p99 by more than half on data it never saw, so every cut is inconclusive at low confidence and stays off the list.

  1. Fixes

    Changes that take time out of SLO off the recorded day, with the minutes and failed requests they remove and what they cost a month.

  2. Verdict and confidence

    Pass, fail or inconclusive, and how far to trust it. Low confidence means the change leans on a service the model doesn't explain well, runs it past anything the history showed, or the model missed badly on data it never saw.

  3. Its full backtest

    Opens the change on the replay screen as a strategy, focused, drawn against the system as built over the whole history.

Incidents

Every stretch the focused strategy spent out of SLO, with its cause.

  1. The tester tabs

    Overview, Incidents, Endpoints, Changes and Model all describe the focused strategy. The count on Incidents is how many stretches it spent out of SLO.

  2. One row per incident

    When it started, how long it lasted, peak p99, peak errors, and why: the busiest component and how close it was to its limit. When nothing was near its limit, the row says so. HotROD's early incidents are its driver's load-dependent errors, not capacity. board ↗ opens that moment as a replay.

Endpoints

Each endpoint is judged by its own SLO and priced by its own path through the system. This is the Astronomy Shop with an autoscaled frontend.

  1. One row per endpoint

    Endpoint ids come from the traces' route templates, so /api/products/{productId} is one endpoint, not thousands.

  2. Its own SLO

    Set per endpoint in the import manifest (model.endpoints.<id>.slo). A checkout that must answer in 800 ms is held to that, not to the system average.

  3. Failed requests

    As built → with the strategy, over the whole history.

  4. Minutes out of its SLO

    Green when the strategy removes time out of SLO, red when it adds it.

  5. $ per million requests

    Compute along the endpoint's critical path, usage-billed requests and data transfer. Here a checkout costs about 17 times a product page ($5.15 vs $0.31 per million) because of the services it calls.

Changes

  1. The strategy, as YAML

    Edit it here and the backtest reruns as you type. The same file works with the CLI and the pull-request check. Every option is listed under strategies.

  2. What changed, and what is assumed

    A plain-English list of every change and every assumption it rests on. For example, a Graviton move without your own benchmark says it assumes equal throughput per vCPU.

Any moment, on the board

Click any moment on the chart, or open on board next to the worst moment. The system opens as a live canvas replaying that stretch of traffic through the model, ten times faster.

  1. The model as a board

    Every component with its fitted capacity, price and replicas, and requests flowing along the call paths from the traces. In-process dependencies (HotROD's simulated MySQL and Redis) sit next to the services that host them. The board is a simplified drawing; the backtest numbers come from the model itself.

  2. Backtest these edits

    Change anything on the board — add a replica, put a cache in front, resize — and press this. The edits come back to the chart as a new strategy, replayed over the whole history.

  3. Back to the chart

    Returns to the backtest without changes.
