Accuracy

How well the backtest predicts reality, measured on three open demo systems run under load in Docker, with their telemetry recorded the way production records it.

How it was measured

Each system ran a 40-minute "day": a night trough, a morning ramp, a lunch peak, a three-minute push above capacity, an evening decline. The model was calibrated on that run only. Then it predicted runs it never saw (the runs, not the method: several calibration rules were added after one of these predictions missed, so treat the table as in-sample for the method, and each check as a single run):

  • the same day with a change really applied (a second replica, an autoscaler, a bigger instance);
  • step load tests up to the breaking point, as built and with the change.

The telemetry was recorded like production: an OpenTelemetry Collector writing 20% of traces and turning every span into request metrics, container metrics scraped by Prometheus every 5 seconds, k6 sending Poisson arrivals and keeping every request. The bill is an AWS Cost and Usage Report built from what actually ran, priced as Fargate in us-east-1.

Where it misses

Each model is also calibrated on the first 70% of its history and asked to predict the rest. Cost holds (0.3–6.3% off). p99 misses badly, by 72–99%, because each history ends with its push above capacity: the last 30% contains an overload the first 70% never came near. With no evidence of where the limit is, the model predicts a healthy evening. A model is only as good as the loads its history has seen, which is why every component's notes say when its capacity is an extrapolation.

On Kubernetes, with a real traffic curve

The Astronomy Shop ran on a kind cluster, observed the way production clusters are: kube-prometheus-stack, ingress-nginx, span metrics and Jaeger. It is a kind cluster on one laptop, not a managed cluster. k6 replayed the arrival curve of two days of NASA's 1995 web log, each compressed into 40 minutes: an ordinary day and the STS-70 launch day at 2.2× the traffic. Five changes to its frontend were answered by verify_change before the day, then made for real. The first version got 2 verdicts right from the ordinary day alone, called 1 inconclusive and got 2 wrong. A plain CPU-ratio rule got 4 right. The misses had one cause. A single-threaded service under a CPU limit collapses well below the limit because of CFS throttling, and extrapolating CPU per request reads that as more capacity than there is. Calibration now fits the share of throttled periods against CPU use and caps capacity where it reaches 25%, the threshold of the CPUThrottlingHigh alert. With that, the ordinary day alone got all 5 right; since a memory-leak term was added it gets 4. That score is in-sample: the throttle rules were designed on these runs.

Out of sample, verdicts on nine more runs were committed to the repository before each run was recorded, in three rounds. Across all of them the model got 3 right, 1 wrong, and said inconclusive on 5 (3 of those leaned the right way). A plain CPU-ratio rule got 6 right and 3 wrong. The model is wrong less often because it abstains more: on hit rate alone it does not beat the rule. Its p99 on each day's own traffic is within 20% of the measured p99 in only 3 of 14 runs; it underestimates busy days. In round 1 it got 3 of 4 right with none wrong, where the rule called a run that failed safe. Under a CPU limit below one core, every endpoint's p99 carries one CFS stall of (1 − limit) × 100 ms, a wait the traces cannot see. The model now takes it out of the baseline and puts it back at the plan's limit. At 1 core its p99 went from 98 to 46 ms, measured 28 (that term was designed on this run). Busy days are still underestimated, because stalls stack once more periods are throttled.

Then a node was stopped for real at minute 19 of the ordinary day. check_node_loss, run afterwards with today's code and the pod placement at the kill (a retrodiction, not a committed prediction), names the seven services that lost their only replica and gives +7 minutes out of SLO. Kubernetes replaced the pods in 348 s, against the 380 s the model allows. The measured outage was still +10.5 minutes: the frontend's gRPC channel to cart stayed broken until the frontend ran out of heap and restarted. The replay times the platform's recovery, not the clients'. Its answer still does; beside it, the node-loss tools now give a sensitivity for gRPC callers that stay broken 252 s after their dependency is back, the time measured in this run. Here that reads +11 minutes, close to the +10.5 measured, but only because the 252 s came from this same run. The full study, with every run, is in reference/k8s/README.md.

A second system on Kubernetes: PetClinic

The throttle rules were designed on the Astronomy Shop, so the model was then checked on a system it was not shaped on: Spring PetClinic Microservices (JVMs, a reactive gateway, the OpenTelemetry Java agent) on the same cluster and the same NASA days. Six questions were chosen after the ordinary day was recorded, and their verdicts were committed before any of the six runs. The model got 4 right, 1 wrong and 1 inconclusive that leaned the wrong way. The plain CPU-ratio rule got 5 right. The model was right about the system as built and about one gateway instead of two (on either day, and as an HPA). It got halving customers-service's CPU limit from 250m to 125m wrong. That broke both days: 29.5 minutes out of SLO on launch day, 4 on the ordinary day. The recording shows the service spent 4.3 ms of CPU per request instead of 1.0 ms. On launch day it dropped abruptly to the warm cost at minute 30.5 and stayed there. That is a JVM finishing its JIT compilation. At 125m the compiler threads lose the throttled CPU to the request threads, so the warm-up that takes minutes at 250m took half an hour under load. Warm, 125m was enough. The steady state the model predicted was right, and the cold start it never modelled was what failed. Its p99 on healthy days was pessimistic: 14 ms against 7–9 ms measured, within 20% in none of the 6 runs. Recomputed with today's model (in sample, the fix found on these runs): 9 ms on healthy days, and within 20% in 4 of 6 runs together with the JIT warm-up below (also in sample), and the ordinary-day holdout off by 4% instead of 58%. Calibration had read the baseline from the quietest minutes of the ordinary day, which were its slowest: an idle gateway path wakes for each request, so its latency falls with load until anything queues. It now reads the baseline from every minute in which nothing on the path queues, weighted by requests. Details: reference/k8s/petclinic/README.md.

The model then learned JIT warm-up. A JVM or .NET pod that starts during a replay costs ×5 the CPU per request until it has done 30 core-seconds of compilation, which it gets only from CPU its requests leave idle. Those numbers were fitted on these two runs, so what follows is a retrodiction, not a score. From the same ordinary day, today's code says fail for launch day (38.5 minutes) and inconclusive, leaning pass for the ordinary day. Rolled out at the day's first, quiet moment, the pod warms in 7.8 minutes. A rollout at a busier moment would not, and the verdict says so. The real run failed on its rollout's own warm-up ramp. The out-of-sample verdicts above stand as committed.

A second round then tested the warm-up on runs it was not fitted on. Three new changes were chosen, and their verdicts committed before any of them ran: visits-service at 125m, customers-service at 175m, and customers-service on an HPA of 1–3, all on launch day. All three passed. The model got 1 right and 2 inconclusive, both leaning pass. The CPU rule got 3 right. visits-service warmed in about 5 minutes against the 7.9 the replay gave. The verdict was withheld anyway, because the system couldn't carry the day's peak while the pod was cold, though this rollout came at a quiet moment. customers-service at 175m showed no cold stretch at all: the warm-up traffic before the run had compiled it. Across both rounds the model got 5 right, 1 wrong and 3 inconclusive, against the rule's 8 right and 1 wrong. Its inconclusives cost more right answers than they saved wrong ones. The two changes that follow (the cold-capacity check becomes a warning, and the compile work is measured from a pod start where the history has one) are made after seeing this round, so a later round has to test them.

On real production telemetry

On the 60 busiest services in Alibaba's 2021 microservices trace, the calibration predicted each service's CPU from its traffic over 3.6 held-out hours (median error per service). Where traffic moved by 15% or more within an hour (44 services), it was off by 5.9%, against 11.0% for CPU per request held constant and 13.2% for "the same as an hour ago". Above anything the training saw (14 services), it was off by 12.9% against 30.4%. Over all minutes, moving or not, it ties "the same as an hour ago" (7.4% each). This checks CPU only, not latency: the trace has no percentiles and these services run far below saturation. Details: reference/alibaba/README.md.

Where you see it

The accuracy record travels with the model: the backtest screen's Model accuracy panel, hindcast accuracy model.json, the footer of every report, pull-request comment and card. For your own data, hindcast calibrate --holdout 0.3 adds the same holdout check.
