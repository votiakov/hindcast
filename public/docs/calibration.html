Backtest on real data

How the model is fitted

From your files, Hindcast builds a model of the system: what each component costs, how much load it takes, how its latency grows. Then it replays your history through the model and shows how well it reproduces what was measured. Components it can't explain are flagged.

The Model tab

The Astronomy Shop, calibrated from 40 minutes of telemetry: 11 services in eight languages.

  1. A flagged component

    The Next.js frontend is marked unexplained: replaying the history through its spans misses its self time by 362%. Its queue sits outside the spans. A busy event loop doesn't accept a request, so no span starts, and clients wait seconds while traced time stays at 18 ms. Hindcast reads its capacity from CPU instead and says so. The model is still right about it: the frontend's 1-core breaking point was predicted within 6.5%.

  2. Fit status

    The whole history replayed through the model, compared bucket by bucket with what was measured: good under 15% error, fair under 30%, poor under 60%, otherwise unexplained. cost only means the component is in the bill or container metrics but never in traces.

  3. Capacity per replica, with its band

    The req/s one replica can serve and its 10th–90th percentile range. The backtest draws from this range, so uncertain capacities show as wider bands on the chart.

  4. What limits it

    CPU (the limit is reached at the fitted CPU per request), n at a time (a lock, connection pool or worker pool), or nothing seen.

  5. Cost from the bill

    Compute spend divided by the replica-hours that actually ran.

  6. Why

    How the capacity was read and what it rests on. "load never approached capacity; the CPU limit is extrapolated" tells you which numbers are a stretch.

  7. Components nobody instrumented

    Valkey has no tracing of its own. The frontend's spans call it (db.system: redis), so it becomes a component with its own load, latency and capacity.

Learned from an overload

PetClinic, a Java app on PostgreSQL. Its history includes a push to 280 req/s that the 2-core JVM didn't survive.

  1. A knee, and a metastable failure

    "broke between 215 and 256 req/s per replica, and while overloaded finished only 145 req/s". Once it tipped over, it stayed down until the backlog was gone. The model keeps both numbers, so a backtest shows the same collapse. Because the CPU ran pinned while it collapsed, the limit counts as CPU: on 4 cores the model predicted a break at 487 req/s, and a stress test measured 470.

  2. Cost only

    The nginx in front has no traces and no request metrics. It is priced from the bill and left out of the latency model.

The fitting rules

Each rule below was added when a recording of a real system broke a prediction. The full list, with formulas, is in docs/calibration.md in the repository.

rulewhat it doeslearned from
Visits and the critical pathTraces are folded into visits: one service handling one call. Self time excludes child visits. The critical path says how many ms of each endpoint every service owns.—
Uninstrumented dependenciesCLIENT spans to databases, caches and queues (db.system, peer.service, messaging.system) become components of their own.Astronomy Shop's Valkey, PetClinic's PostgreSQL

Sampling driftThe real share a sampler kept is measured against request metrics; trace-only rates are rescaled.a 20% sampler kept 20.7%
CPU per requestA regression of CPU on load, with its standard error.—
Capacity: none, CPU, pool or lockEach explanation is replayed through the measured history, backlog included; the one that reproduces the measured self time best wins. A lock is c holders each held s ms: capacity c / s.HotROD's MySQL mutex
Service-time variabilityWaits scale with how variable service times are (Allen–Cunneen), fitted rather than assumed.Poisson arrivals on near-constant service times
Seen while overloadedWhat finished per second while a service was saturated is its capacity.HotROD
Queues outside the spansWhen CPU hit its limit, capacity comes from what finished while pinned and from CPU efficiency, and the hidden queue's steepness is read from end-to-end latency.the Node.js frontend
Knees and metastable failuresAn overload in the history brackets the limit. A rate counts as handled only if the bucket after it was healthy too. A collapse that pinned the CPU scales with cores.PetClinic
Errors that grow with loadA line through the buckets that weren't overloaded.HotROD's driver
Skewed latencyEach endpoint is lognormal through its own p50 and p99; a system p99 is a quantile of the traffic-weighted mix. Tails are fitted on everyday buckets, not overloads."mostly 3 ms, rarely 300 ms"
Extrapolation is a rangeWhen the history never came near a limit and CPU and latency disagree, the band spans both.Astronomy Shop's ad service
Long historiesOver 1,000 buckets, capacity is fitted on a compact copy: the busiest episodes with the buckets after them (where a backlog drains), and a sample of the rest. Fit quality is still measured on the whole history.two weeks of 1-minute buckets took minutes
Memory leaksMemory is fitted against the requests a container has served since it started, as well as against its load. When served requests explain it far better (R² ≥ 0.8, at least 0.2 above load), it is a leak: the replay counts each container's requests and OOM-kills it at its limit, so a busier day is killed sooner.Astronomy Shop's email: +9.6 MiB per 1,000 requests on an ordinary day, killed on every launch day; predicted out of sample at a 1.5× day (minute 29.5 predicted, 35 observed)
Holdout by requestsAccuracy is measured on the last 30% of the requests, not of the time.a day idle but for one busy hour
Cost$ per replica-hour from compute spend over replica-hours actually run; per request from usage-billed lines; transfer from placement at AWS rates (cross-zone $0.01/GB each way, cross-region $0.02/GB, internet $0.09/GB).—

What it doesn't do

  • A limit the history never approached is an extrapolation; the band shows how wide it is. One overload in the history is worth more than a month of quiet days.
  • Cold starts after a deploy (JIT warm-up, empty caches) are not part of the backtest.
  • Errors unrelated to load (a bad deploy, a bug) are a baseline rate, not predicted.
  • A garbage-collected runtime's p99 over 5 minutes can swing 5× at the same load. The model predicts the distribution, not which window a pause lands in.
