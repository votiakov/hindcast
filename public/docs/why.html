Why Hindcast

What it is for, how it differs from the system-design simulators you may have seen, why you can check its numbers, and what it can't do.

What it is for

Hindcast answers one question before a change ships: what would this have done to our real traffic? A resize, an autoscaler, a cache, a replica, a retry policy, Graviton, a Kubernetes manifest diff, a Terraform plan. It builds a model of your system from what it already exports — Prometheus metrics, OpenTelemetry traces, the cloud bill — replays the change over the history, and says what it would have cost in money and in minutes out of SLO, with how far to trust the answer.

Around that sit the tools the same people need on a whiteboard: a simulator to design and break systems, a failure audit that tries every failure and prices every fix, lessons and comparisons to learn from, and an MCP server so an AI agent can do the work itself.

"Isn't this a Breakscale clone?"

Half of it looks like one, and that is a fair first read. Breakscale is a good system-design simulator: draw components, push traffic, watch queues fill and retries storm. So are PaperDraw, systemInPain, Chinilla and others; "drag boxes, press play" is a genre, and Hindcast's Sandbox, Lessons and Live views are in it, with the genre's usual parts (chaos, retries, timeouts, breakers, seeded runs, a request tracer, lessons, exports; see sandbox and lessons). If learning by breaking diagrams is what you want, Breakscale is free, open source and good at it; use it.

The other half is why Hindcast exists. A diagram's numbers are the ones you typed, so it can tell you where a design breaks but not whether your frontend would have held last week's peak on one replica fewer. Hindcast reads those numbers out of your Prometheus, traces and bill, replays the change over the traffic you had, and gives a verdict whose track record is published: 18 questions on two systems, each answer committed before the real run, 8 right, 2 wrong, 8 inconclusive. The board shares that engine, which is why any moment of a backtest opens on it.

Hindcast's two engines are its own: a fluid one that treats traffic as flow rates, and a discrete-event one that simulates each request. Both are written in Rust and checked against each other in CI; on 200 generated boards their error rates agree within 5 points on 199, and on the 98 that both serve healthily their p50 and p99 agree within the discrete-event engine's seed-to-seed range ±25% on 97 (docs/engines.md). Where the boards look alike, it's because load balancers, caches and queues look alike.

Why you can check the numbers

Every accuracy figure comes from reference systems in the repository, and the committed data regenerates the tables from a clone. The main one is the OpenTelemetry Astronomy Shop on a kind cluster on one laptop, with k6 replaying the arrival curve of NASA's July 1995 web log (accuracy, reference/k8s). Before each of nine out-of-sample runs, the model's verdict was committed; then the run was recorded and scored. Because the model's rules were shaped on that system, a second one followed: Spring PetClinic (Java services) on the same cluster, nine more questions in two rounds, committed the same way.

The result, plainly. Astronomy Shop: 6 right, 1 wrong, 8 inconclusive; a plain CPU-ratio rule got 10 right and 5 wrong. PetClinic: 5 right, 1 wrong, 3 inconclusive; the rule got 8 right and 1 wrong. Together, 11 right, 2 wrong and 11 inconclusive of 24, against the rule's 18 right and 6 wrong. The model is wrong less often because it says when a change goes past anything the history showed, not because it is right more often, and those abstentions don't yet sort passes from failures: of the 11, 6 passed for real and 5 failed. Both of its wrong calls, the rule got wrong too. PetClinic's miss was a Java service at half its CPU limit that never finished warming up; the model has since learned JIT warm-up, but its parameters were fitted on that miss, and the round after it (1 right, 2 inconclusive, 0 wrong) is the only out-of-sample test of it so far. The Shop's p99 on each day's own traffic is within 20% in 3 of 14 runs, and it underestimates busy days. Two committed predictions about a memory leak came true: a kill (predicted at minute 29.5, it came at 35) and, after a change, no kill. The leak was found in an ordinary day after earlier runs had shown the kills, so the rest of the leak table is a retrodiction with today's code. What it gets wrong is listed in the same tables.

The three Docker demo systems tell the same story from the other side. Calibrated on one 40-minute day, the model predicted runs with a change really applied: p99 within 3–10%, breaking points within 0–14%. Asked for the end of its own history, which held an overload the earlier part never reached, it missed p99 by 72–99%. It is only as good as the loads its history has seen.

The one check on real production telemetry is narrower: on the 60 busiest services of Alibaba's public 2021 microservices trace, predicting CPU from traffic over 3.6 held-out hours. Where traffic moved, it was off by 5.9% against 11–13% for two naive forecasts; on steady minutes it ties "the same as an hour ago". That tests CPU per request, not latency or verdicts.

"Why not just run a load test?"

Run one when you can; Hindcast says when a change goes past what your history can vouch for. A load test measures one change, at the load you chose, on an environment you had to build to look like production. A backtest replays many changes over the traffic you actually had, in seconds, with the money attached, and needs nothing but the telemetry you already keep. It can't see past that history: a change that runs a service beyond any load it has met comes back low confidence, load-test before trusting it, and a pass at low confidence is not called safe. A load test's telemetry can also be part of the history it calibrates on. On the Astronomy Shop that did not help: calibrated on the ordinary day plus a 12-minute load test, the model got 1 right, 2 wrong and 6 inconclusive on its nine out-of-sample runs (reference/k8s).

"Queueing models never match real systems."

Partly true, and the record shows where. The model picks, per service, whichever explanation replays its own history best: no limit, CPU-bound, or a lock or pool that serves a fixed number of requests at a time (an M/M/c queue), with throttling, memory and nodes on top. Capacity holds up best: breaking points within 14% on the demo systems. The tail is weakest: p99 on each Kubernetes day's own traffic is within 20% in only 3 of 14 runs, too low on busy days. Under a CPU limit the kernel's scheduler (CFS) pauses a container that has spent its quota until the next 100 ms period; on busy days those pauses stack in ways the model doesn't reproduce. The discrete-event engine simulates every request, so retry storms and timeouts emerge instead of being assumed, but it is still a model of the services, fitted from what they exported.

"How is it different from a right-sizing recommender?"

A right-sizing tool in general reads usage and suggests requests and limits. Hindcast takes a proposed change, including such a suggestion, and replays it over the recorded traffic through capacity, throttling, OOM kills and the node pool, and answers in minutes out of SLO and money, or says it can't tell. It doesn't run continuously, watch the cluster or apply anything.

"We don't run Prometheus."

It reads Prometheus (with presets for OpenTelemetry span metrics, HTTP server metrics, Spring, ingress-nginx, Istio, Linkerd, cAdvisor and kube-state-metrics), Datadog, CloudWatch get-metric-data output, and CSV in long or wide form (a Grafana panel export). Traces come from Jaeger or Tempo, or as OTLP or Jaeger JSON files; bills as AWS CUR or a GCP billing export. On Datadog, hindcast quickstart --datadog <site> reads APM trace metrics, the Agent's Kubernetes metrics and ingress-nginx through the Metrics API, with who calls whom from APM service dependencies; the keys stay in environment variables. It loses call counts, nodes and autoscalers against Prometheus with traces, and it has been tested against a mock built from Datadog's API documentation only, not a live account (details). There is no New Relic connector: its metrics go in through a CSV export (bring your data).

"Why Rust and WebAssembly?"

So one engine runs everywhere it is used: in the browser for the app, which needs no server and works on your data without uploading it, and as one native binary for the CLI, the MCP server and the agent tools. Speed is the other reason: the discrete-event engine simulates every request (the shop example's 90 seconds of Black Friday is about a million events, in a tenth of a second), and the fluid one runs a simulated second in about a millisecond, which is what makes Monte Carlo runs, the failure audit and 30 draws per strategy over weeks of history practical on a laptop. The cost is a Rust toolchain to build the binary from source.

Is it open source?

Yes: Apache-2.0, the engine, the CLI, the MCP server, the app, the lessons and the reference data alike. Everything on these pages can be run and checked from a clone.

What it touches in your systems

Nothing, beyond reading. It queries Prometheus, and Jaeger or Tempo if you have them (traces are optional), reads exported files and the bill, and runs kubectl get only when asked. Every answer is computed on your machine or in your browser. There is no analytics or telemetry. When an AI agent runs the tools, their output goes to that agent's model provider, as with any tool it uses.

What it doesn't do

  • Its evidence so far is demo systems and a laptop cluster, not a production fleet; the one production check (Alibaba's trace) covers CPU per request only.
  • It doesn't replace a load test for a change far past anything your history shows — it says so (confidence low, "load-test before trusting it"). On the reference runs those answers came back on changes that passed about as often as on ones that failed, so an inconclusive is a reason to test, not a forecast of failure.
  • Its recovery times are the platform's. Callers that stay broken after a dependency restarts (a stuck gRPC channel) are a stated assumption, not a fit: a link can carry reconnect (seconds, or until the caller restarts) in the simulator, and where a lost service is called over gRPC, node-loss checks show beside their answer what it costs if those callers stay broken 252 s more, the one time measured on the reference cluster. It has no term yet for a container stalling before an OOM kill.
  • Its bands show how much the answer moves with the fitted capacities, not how wrong the model itself can be; they aren't calibrated against outcomes yet, and a fixed replica count has no cost band at all, because its cost doesn't depend on load.
  • It models what your telemetry shows. A service nobody instruments is a guess, labelled as one.
  • The simulator's built-in capacities are teaching defaults; calibrate them, or use a real pricing profile and your own numbers.

Who it is for

  • Platform engineers and SREs: verify a change before it ships, review capacity, find the single points of failure, gate pull requests.
  • CTOs and leads: what a decision costs and what it risks, in one page, with its basis.
  • Engineers learning system design: lessons, comparisons and a sandbox where every number has an explanation, and real systems to start from.
